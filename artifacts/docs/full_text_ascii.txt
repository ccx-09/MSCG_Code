MSCG: A Controlled Multi-Scale Benchmark for
Robust Segmentation under Scale and Corruption Shift
Abstract
Robust image segmentation requires models that remain reliable when target scale
and image quality vary together. Existing robustness benchmarks usually stress
corruption type, while standard segmentation datasets rarely provide explicit
control over geometric scale. We introduce the Multi-Scale Complexity Grid
(MSCG), a semi-synthetic benchmark that organizes 676 images by target scale,
image condition, and corruption severity under an oracle point-prompting proto-
col. MSCG is used to compare three segmentation paradigms: SAM fine-tuned
with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), DeepLabV3+, and a VGG16+XGBoost
classical pipeline.
In a matched five-fold evaluation, SAM+LoRA achieves
the highest mIoU (76.49% ? 2.72%), outperforming DeepLabV3+ (66.69% ?
1.85%) and the classical pipeline (51.50% ? 5.19%). The largest performance
changes occur across geometric scales rather than corruption severities, indicating
that scale remains the dominant stressor in this controlled setting. A separate
efficiency comparison shows that rank-16 LoRA reaches 98.6% of full fine-tuning
mIoU while updating 1.25% of parameters; this comparison is reported descrip-
tively because full fine-tuning uses three folds whereas the LoRA rank ablation
uses five folds. The results position MSCG as an interpretable benchmark for
diagnosing segmentation robustness and deployment trade-offs.
Keywords: Multi-Scale Complexity Grid, Robust segmentation benchmark,

1. Introduction
Foundation segmentation models (Bommasani et al., 2021) such as SAM (Kir-
illov et al., 2023) have changed the accuracy and interaction profile of image
segmentation. Instead of training a task-specific model from scratch, a user can
prompt a large pre-trained model and obtain high-quality masks across a broad
range of visual scenes. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning with LoRA (Hu et al.,
2022) further makes this family of models practical to adapt, because most pre-
trained weights remain frozen while a small number of low-rank parameters are
optimized.
These developments are attractive for image-analysis pipelines in
which annotation is expensive, hardware budgets are uneven, or downstream ap-
plications need fast adaptation rather than full model retraining.
Despite this progress, the behavior of foundation segmentation models is still
difficult to interpret when target scale and image quality vary together. Segmen-
tation failures are rarely caused by a single nuisance factor in isolation. A small
target may lose boundary evidence after blur; a large target may exceed the use-
ful locality of a single prompt; noise and fog may affect foreground localization
differently depending on the amount of visible context. Standard segmentation
datasets such as COCO (Lin et al., 2014) and PASCAL VOC (Everingham et al.,
2010) provide rich object and scene diversity, but their natural variation is not
organized as an explicit factorial control over target scale and corruption sever-
ity. Conversely, corruption benchmarks such as ImageNet-C (Hendrycks and Di-
etterich, 2019) made image-quality stress testing systematic, but were designed
primarily for image classification rather than mask-level segmentation. As a re-
sult, it remains hard to determine whether an observed segmentation gap reflects
the model family, the adaptation strategy, target scale, corruption severity, or their
interaction.
Recent studies have begun to examine SAM robustness directly (Wang et al.,
2024; Chen et al., 2024). They show that promptable segmentation can be sensi-
tive to degraded images and that training on corrupted images can improve some
robustness properties. However, these studies do not provide a compact segmen-
tation benchmark that jointly organizes geometric scale, corruption type, and cor-
ruption severity while keeping the prompting protocol fixed. This missing control
matters for model selection. A practitioner choosing between a promptable foun-
dation model, a modern CNN, and a more classical feature-and-classifier pipeline
needs more than a single aggregate mIoU: the decision also depends on whether
the target objects are small or large, whether image quality is expected to degrade,
and whether training cost, storage footprint, or inference latency is the binding
constraint.
This paper addresses that gap with the Multi-Scale Complexity Grid (MSCG),
a controlled benchmark for diagnosing segmentation behavior under scale and
corruption shifts. MSCG is not intended to replace large natural datasets; rather,
it complements them by making selected nuisance factors visible and reportable.
We compare three representative paradigms, SAM+LoRA, DeepLabV3+, and
2
VGG16+XGBoost, under a shared oracle point-prompting protocol. The oracle
prompt isolates mask prediction under controlled visual complexity, so the study
evaluates segmentation behavior rather than prompt-generation quality. The anal-
ysis keeps five forms of evidence separate: the matched five-fold comparison, the
descriptive full fine-tuning versus LoRA efficiency comparison, the scale diagnos-
tic, the endpoint-retention analysis, and the inference benchmark.
The main contributions are:
C1: Controlled complexity grid. We introduce MSCG, a semi-synthetic
benchmark with 676 structured images organized by five geometric scales (from
<1% to >30% of image area), five encoded corruption severity levels, and four
image conditions (clean plus Gaussian blur, shot noise, and fog). The design
supports separate reporting of scale and corruption effects.

Through these experiments, we investigate three research questions that follow
the structure used throughout the paper:
RQ1 (Performance Advantage). Does SAM+LoRA significantly outper-
form other methods across the matched complexity-grid comparison?
RQ2 (Cost-Benefit Trade-off). Compared to full fine-tuning, does LoRA
provide a useful balance between adaptation cost and segmentation accuracy for
practical deployment?
RQ3 (Failure Modes). How do the tested methods behave across scale bins
and corruption endpoints under the controlled MSCG protocol?

2. Related work
2.1. Segmentation Paradigms
Segmentation methods have moved from handcrafted or fixed-feature
pipelines to end-to-end CNNs and, more recently, promptable foundation models.
Classical pipelines commonly combine a feature extractor such as VGG16 (Si-
monyan and Zisserman, 2015) with lightweight tree-ensemble classifiers,
including Random Forests (Breiman, 2001) and XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin,
2016).
Such pipelines remain useful as transparent and relatively modular
baselines: features, classifier, and post-processing can be inspected separately,
and CPU execution is possible when GPU deployment is unavailable.
Their
limitation is that the representation is largely fixed once the feature extractor has
been selected, so boundary errors under scale or image-quality shift are difficult
to correct without redesigning the pipeline.
CNN architectures provide a stronger learned alternative for dense predic-
tion. FCN (Long et al., 2015) established end-to-end semantic segmentation by
converting classification networks into fully convolutional predictors, while U-
Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) emphasized encoder?decoder skip connections for
accurate localization. Subsequent designs introduced stronger multi-scale and
contextual modeling, including Mask R-CNN (He et al., 2017), PSPNet (Zhao
et al., 2017), RefineNet (Lin et al., 2017), and DeepLabV3+ (Chen et al., 2018).
DeepLabV3+ is a useful modern baseline in this study because it combines atrous
spatial pyramid pooling with a lightweight decoder, making it representative of
high-throughput CNN segmentation under a conventional supervised training pro-
tocol.
The third paradigm is promptable foundation segmentation. Unlike a closed-
set CNN trained only to map an image to a class-specific mask, SAM (Kirillov
et al., 2023) uses prompts to specify the region to segment and can be adapted
to new image distributions. This changes the evaluation question. The issue is
no longer only whether a model can learn MSCG masks, but also how much
performance is retained when a large pre-trained segmentation model is adapted
with a small trainable module and tested under controlled shifts. MSCG compares
a classical pipeline, a modern CNN, and a parameter-efficient SAM variant under
identical scale, corruption, and split definitions. The benchmark is used to clarify
where each family is strong, rather than to present a single model as uniformly
preferable.

2.2. Foundation Models and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning
SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023) is a prompt-based segmentation model built on a
Vision Transformer image encoder (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021), which in turn builds
on self-attention (Vaswani et al., 2017). Its design separates image encoding,
prompt encoding, and mask decoding, allowing points, boxes, or masks to act
as spatial queries. The broader foundation-model landscape also includes vision-
language pre-training such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), but the present study
focuses on promptable segmentation rather than cross-modal retrieval or zero-shot
classification. MSCG evaluates mask quality under a controlled prompt protocol;
it does not evaluate open-vocabulary recognition or semantic transfer.
Adapting a large foundation model by updating all parameters can be expen-
sive. Full fine-tuning of a ViT-H segmentation model requires substantial mem-
ory, training time, and checkpoint storage, which may be inappropriate when the
downstream dataset is modest in size. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning offers an
alternative. LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) freezes pre-trained weights and injects low-
rank matrices into attention layers, substantially reducing trainable parameters
while preserving most of the original representation. Prefix-tuning (Li and Liang,
2021), SAM-Adapter (Chen et al., 2023), adaptive-rank variants such as GaRA-
SAM (Lee et al., 2025), and recent unifying PEFT analyses (Mai et al., 2025) all
point to the same practical issue: for foundation models, adaptation cost is part of
model quality.
The present work does not propose a new adapter. It deliberately uses fixed-
rank LoRA as a representative PEFT mechanism so that the evaluation can focus
on behavior under controlled complexity factors. This choice also makes the full
fine-tuning comparison interpretable as an engineering trade-off. The relevant
question is whether a small trainable module can recover most of the segmentation
accuracy while reducing training time and storage. Because the full fine-tuning
and LoRA rank-ablation protocols use different fold counts in our artifacts, we
report that comparison descriptively and avoid treating it as a matched statistical
test.
2.3. Robustness Assessment
Robustness benchmarks such as ImageNet-C (Hendrycks and Dietterich,
2019), which extend the ImageNet evaluation tradition (Deng et al., 2009),
made corruption evaluation standard. Natural-adversarial and test-set replication
studies further exposed generalization gaps in image classifiers (Hendrycks et al.,
2021; Recht et al., 2019). Related analyses of texture and shape bias show that
robustness failures can reflect the visual cues learned by CNNs rather than only
5
the amount of training data (Geirhos et al., 2019). These works established the
value of stress testing, but classification robustness does not directly answer
mask-level questions such as boundary preservation, foreground localization, or
prompt sensitivity.
For segmentation, robustness assessment needs to account for both the im-
age degradation and the geometry of the target. A model can remain accurate on
large, high-contrast objects while failing on small objects under mild blur, or it
can segment small clean objects well but lose coherence when a single prompt is
placed inside a very large mask. SAM-specific studies have shown that prompt-
able segmentation can be sensitive to corruptions (Wang et al., 2024), and Robust-
SAM (Chen et al., 2024) improves robustness by training on degraded images.
These studies motivate corruption-aware segmentation, but they do not jointly
control target scale and corruption severity in a compact benchmark designed for
cross-paradigm comparison.
Recent Image and Vision Computing studies also show why robustness claims
in applied vision should be tied to explicit visual conditions and evaluation pro-
tocols. Yang and Wang studied aerial-imagery risk inspection in which semantic
segmentation must operate under complex scenes and varying risk-region scales,
while Nita et al. used visible-to-SWIR synthesis to evaluate detection robustness
under low-data domain shift (Yang and Wang, 2026; Nita et al., 2026). These
works are domain-specific rather than MSCG-style segmentation benchmarks, but
they reinforce the need to report what visual factors a robustness result actually
covers.
MSCG fills this evaluation gap by providing a controlled grid for diagnos-
ing existing methods rather than a new training objective. Its role is closer to
an evaluation lens: it separates scale bins, image conditions, severity encodings,
and model families so that performance patterns can be connected to observable
factors. This framing also keeps the terminology disciplined. We use ?complex-
ity? to refer to the benchmark?s explicit scale and degradation controls, not to an
unrestricted notion of scene difficulty. We use ?robustness? descriptively for mea-
sured behavior under the tested MSCG factors, not as a claim about all possible
distribution shifts.
3. Multi-Scale Complexity Grid
3.1. MSCG Construction
The Multi-Scale Complexity Grid (MSCG) generates semi-synthetic images
by superimposing real object instances from COCO onto backgrounds with con-
6
trolled scale and image-condition factors. This design varies the two factors exam-
ined in this study, object geometric scale and image corruption, while preserving
natural object appearance. Lighting, viewpoint, and multi-object interactions are
not explicitly modeled; this omission defines the scope of the benchmark rather
than an assumption about real-world scenes. Future work may extend the setup
by incorporating additional sources of variation.
The construction follows a benchmark-first principle. Rather than increasing
scene realism in an unconstrained way, MSCG fixes the main nuisance factors that
are difficult to separate in natural datasets. Each image contains one foreground
instance, so the evaluation focuses on foreground localization and boundary pre-
diction without confounding effects from instance overlap, object counting, or
ambiguous instance assignment. This single-instance design is a limitation for
real-scene deployment, but it is useful for diagnosing whether a segmentation
method changes behavior when the target occupies different fractions of the im-
age or when image quality is degraded in a controlled manner. In this sense,
MSCG is intended as a diagnostic grid rather than a replacement for large-scale
natural-image segmentation benchmarks.

3.2. Corruptions, Dataset and Splits
Following ImageNet-C (Hendrycks and Dietterich, 2019), we define four im-
age conditions: clean images and three corruptions (Gaussian blur, shot noise,
fog), evaluated over five severity levels (k0?k4) in the matched five-fold experi-
ments. A complementary validation severity-endpoint analysis, used only for the
descriptive corruption-retention analysis, reports the endpoint as k5. The process-
ing workflow has four steps:
1. COCO object instances are filtered.
2. Synthetic backgrounds are generated at pre-defined scale levels.
3. Specified corruptions are applied.
4. Outputs are saved in COCO format.
The MSCG dataset used in this study contains 676 images organized across
a structured scale ? condition ? severity design. Each image contains a single
foreground instance composited onto a controlled background; no multi-instance
overlap or occlusion is introduced. A representative sample of the dataset, show-
ing its coverage of scales, distortion types, and severity levels, is shown in Fig-
ure 1. The dataset is smaller than conventional large-scale segmentation bench-
marks and does not model lighting or viewpoint changes. Its purpose is instead
controlled diagnosis: the images are stratified by five scale bins, four image-
condition categories, and encoded degradation severity, while five-fold stratifi-
cation preserves the joint distribution of these factors. Each fold contains approx-
imately 135 images, which provides sufficient power for paired t-tests to detect
large effects (Cohen?s d ?1.5) at the corrected significance level of ? = 0.0167
8
with power > 0.80. Instance-level grouping keeps each original COCO instance
in a single fold, preventing leakage between training and test sets.
The split strategy addresses a common risk in semi-synthetic datasets: related
composites can inflate performance when they appear in both training and test par-
titions. MSCG groups images at the source-instance level before fold assignment.
Corrupted variants and scale-conditioned composites derived from the same orig-
inal instance are kept within a single fold whenever they would otherwise leak
instance-specific appearance cues. The resulting protocol gives every compared
method the same fold boundaries and the same held-out images, which is a pre-
requisite for the paired statistical analysis reported later. The benchmark design
connects the dataset construction, the evaluation protocol, and the interpretation
of the paired tests.

4. Methods
4.1. Classical Baseline and Modern CNN Baseline
The classical baseline uses a pre-trained VGG16 network (Simonyan and Zis-
serman, 2015) as a fixed feature extractor, following the common transfer-learning
use of pre-trained CNN representations (Tan et al., 2018). Each input image is di-
vided into overlapping 64?64 pixel patches with a stride of 32, and features from
multiple layers are aggregated for patch representation. An XGBoost classifier
then performs binary foreground/background classification for each patch, and the
patch-level outputs are reconstructed into a pixel-wise probability map. A Dense
Conditional Random Field (Dense CRF) is used to improve spatial consistency
along segmentation boundaries (Kr?henb?hl and Koltun, 2011).
The modern CNN baseline, DeepLabV3+ (Chen et al., 2018), uses a ResNet-
101 backbone (He et al., 2016), an atrous spatial pyramid pooling module, and
a lightweight decoder for multi-scale feature fusion and upsampling. The model
contains approximately 59 million trainable parameters and is trained end-to-end.
Training uses AdamW with an initial learning rate of 1e-4 and a cosine annealing
scheduler. The loss combines binary cross-entropy and Dice loss, with standard
data augmentation. During inference, input images are resized to 512?512, and
the output masks are upsampled to the original 1024?1024 resolution.
These two baselines are included for different reasons. VGG16+XGBoost
gives a modular, CPU-oriented reference in which feature extraction, classifi-
cation, and boundary smoothing are separated. DeepLabV3+ gives a stronger
end-to-end CNN reference with learned multi-scale context and much lower in-
ference latency than SAM+LoRA. Together they bracket the comparison between
a classical fixed-feature pipeline, a conventional dense-prediction network, and a
promptable foundation model adapted with a small trainable module.
4.2. SAM+LoRA: Foundation Model with Efficient Fine-Tuning
SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023) consists of a ViT-H image encoder, a prompt en-
coder, and a mask decoder. We use a point-based prompting strategy: given the
ground-truth mask, a positive point is placed at its centroid and a negative point
is placed outside the corresponding bounding box. This is an oracle-prompting
setting. It evaluates mask prediction under ideal prompt placement and isolates
the effect of the MSCG image factors on segmentation quality. In practical de-
ployment, approximate prompts could instead be obtained from object detectors
or human interaction.

For method comparison, we adopt hierarchical multi-fold cross-validation, a

The statistical protocol is used only for the matched three-way comparison.
Full-parameter fine-tuning versus LoRA rank ablation, validation endpoint-
retention, inference timing, and qualitative attention inspection are reported as
separate descriptive analyses because their fold structure or measurement purpose
differs from the matched five-fold comparison. Keeping these analyses separate
avoids treating all tables as if they shared the same inferential status. In particular,
a descriptive efficiency comparison can inform deployment decisions without
becoming a statistical equivalence test.
5.2. Implementation Details
The general software environment for all experiments included: Python 3.10,
PyTorch 2.1.0 (with CUDA 11.8 and cuDNN 8.6), XGBoost 2.0.3, and Albumen-
tations 1.3.0. To ensure reproducibility, the random seeds for Python, NumPy,
PyTorch, and CUDA were fixed at 42 across all runs.
Hardware configurations varied slightly based on the computational require-
ments of different training paradigms. Inference speed was evaluated on a single
NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB VRAM) using a dedicated benchmarking script
provided in this work. The evaluation protocol was as follows: a batch size of 1
was used, preceded by 10 warm-up runs, followed by 100 timed inference itera-
tions for each method. Explicit CUDA synchronization was enforced to ensure
accurate timing measurements.
Full-parameter fine-tuning experiments were conducted on a server equipped
with a 48GB vGPU, using an input resolution of 1024?1024. LoRA rank ab-
lation (r ?{4, 8, 16}) was performed on a single-GPU workstation featuring an
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 (33.7GB). All configurations were trained on the
MSCG dataset following a unified protocol.
For each training run, we recorded start and end timestamps using the system
clock, extracted the best validation mIoU from training logs, and estimated storage
footprint from checkpoint file sizes. Throughput (images/second) was calculated
based on training duration and iteration count. These measurements provide the
basis for the efficiency analysis presented in Section 6.2.
All timing measurements are interpreted as implementation-level evidence un-
der the stated hardware and software settings. They are not normalized across un-
related hardware platforms, and they are not used to infer algorithmic complexity.
13
The inference benchmark is reported together with the exact batch size, warm-
up count, timed iterations, and synchronization procedure, while the training-
efficiency table is used to compare the observed adaptation cost of full fine-tuning
and LoRA within the recorded experimental environment.

7. Discussion
The MSCG experiments show that aggregate segmentation accuracy is only
one part of the model-selection problem. Scale, adaptation cost, inference latency,
and prompt protocol all change how the same accuracy numbers should be read.
21
7.1. Interpreting the Robustness Pattern
The MSCG results suggest that the strongest stressor in this controlled setting
is geometric scale rather than the tested corruption severities. The separate effi-
ciency comparison also shows that a rank-16 LoRA adapter, updating 1.25% of
the parameters, can approach full-fine-tuning mIoU, although the full-fine-tuning
and LoRA summaries use different fold protocols. These findings indicate that
useful segmentation behavior can be retained under parameter-efficient adapta-
tion, but they do not prove an architectural mechanism. This reading is consistent
with Li et al. (Li et al., 2025), who discuss the relation between fine-tuning, accu-
racy, and robustness, while leaving mechanism-level confirmation to future work
with controlled representation analyses.
The scale pattern also clarifies what MSCG contributes as an evaluation tool.
A single aggregate score would identify SAM+LoRA as the strongest method, but
it would hide the change in relative difficulty across area-ratio bins. The largest-
target regime is especially informative because it challenges the assumption that
larger objects are always easier. Under the oracle point-prompt protocol, a sin-
gle positive point provides strong evidence that an object is present, but it does
not describe the full extent of a mask that occupies a large fraction of the image.
MSCG makes this behavior visible without attributing it to a single architectural
cause. The result should be read as a measured pattern that motivates more de-
tailed prompt and representation analyses, not as a complete explanation of why
the pattern occurs.
The corruption-retention result needs the same boundary. The endpoint anal-
ysis indicates that SAM+LoRA retained most of its clean-image mIoU at the
strongest evaluated endpoint in that validation trajectory. This supports the nar-
rower claim that the adapted model did not collapse under the tested corruptions.
It does not cover every corruption type or deployment environment. Within the
controlled MSCG setting, scale accounts for a larger observed performance range
than the tested degradation endpoint.
7.2. Deployment Considerations
The three paradigms occupy different operating regions. SAM+LoRA gives
the best mask quality in the matched MSCG comparison and is most appropri-
ate when segmentation accuracy is the main constraint but full fine-tuning is too
costly. DeepLabV3+ gives lower mIoU but reaches 66.7 FPS, making it the more
plausible choice when throughput dominates. VGG16+XGBoost remains a CPU-
oriented reference, but its accuracy gap on MSCG is large enough that it should
mainly be viewed as a fallback when GPU-based methods are unavailable.
22
The practical choice depends on the target scale distribution, image-quality
conditions, and operational budget. If annotation and adaptation time are limiting
costs, LoRA provides a compact way to adapt SAM while avoiding the memory
and storage burden of full fine-tuning. If real-time throughput is the main con-
straint and a moderate reduction in mIoU is acceptable, DeepLabV3+ is the more
practical option. If the expected deployment images contain many large target
masks, the scale analysis suggests that aggregate validation accuracy should be
checked against scale-specific behavior before a model is selected.
7.3. Limitations and Scope
The MSCG benchmark is designed for controlled natural-image segmentation,
so its conclusions should not be read as evidence of semantic transfer to safety-
critical domains such as medical imaging. Geometric robustness under the MSCG
protocol does not by itself establish domain generalization; domain-specific grids
and targeted adaptation methods, including pixel-level domain transfer with cross-
domain consistency (Chen et al., 2019), remain necessary when the source and
target domains differ substantially.
The evaluation framework also has design limits. MSCG uses three corrup-
tion types, Gaussian blur, shot noise, and fog, so it does not cover the full range
of ImageNet-C-style degradations such as motion blur, JPEG compression, or
weather effects. The LoRA evaluation uses fixed ranks (r ?{4, 8, 16}); input-
aware gated-rank adaptation (Lee et al., 2025) may change the scale-vs-corruption
pattern. The foundation-model comparison is also limited to SAM with a ViT-H
encoder. Applying the same grid to SAM 2 (Ravi et al., 2024), SegFormer (Xie
et al., 2021), and Mask2Former (Cheng et al., 2022) would test whether the ob-
served behavior is specific to SAM+LoRA or broader across segmentation archi-
tectures. Finally, MSCG contains single-instance, non-occluded images. Over-
lapping objects and cluttered scenes remain outside the present scope.
The present analysis also focuses on prompt-conditioned segmentation quality
under an oracle point setting. This choice isolates the mask-prediction component,
but it does not evaluate how prompts would be generated in a fully automatic sys-
tem. Detector-derived prompts, interactive corrections, and multi-point prompt-
ing may change both aggregate accuracy and scale-dependent behavior. Future
work should couple MSCG with prompt-generation protocols and test whether
the same scale patterns persist when prompts are noisy, automatically generated,
or supplied through user interaction.
23
8. Conclusion
This study introduced the Multi-Scale Complexity Grid (MSCG), a semi-
synthetic benchmark for assessing segmentation behavior under controlled
variation in target scale and image condition.
Using this benchmark, we
compared three representative approaches: SAM+LoRA, DeepLabV3+, and a
VGG16+XGBoost classical pipeline.
SAM+LoRA achieved the highest mIoU in the matched MSCG compari-
son, whereas DeepLabV3+ provided the strongest inference-latency profile and
the classical pipeline remained substantially less accurate.
The separate full-
parameter fine-tuning comparison showed that rank-16 LoRA reached 98.6% of
full fine-tuning mIoU while updating only 1.25% of parameters, supporting its
practical value as a parameter-efficient adaptation strategy.
Future work should extend MSCG to multi-instance scenes, adaptive prompt-
ing strategies, and domain-specific evaluation protocols. Applying the same pro-
tocol to SAM 2 (Ravi et al., 2024) and other successor foundation models, and
expanding the corruption taxonomy toward the full ImageNet-C suite, would test
whether the scale-dependent patterns observed here persist under broader condi-
tions.
